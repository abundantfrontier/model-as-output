# Chapter 1 — To List

*Day 1. Layer 1.*

The ship made noise the whole way. Air moved in the vents. Ice ticked in the hull. Diane was in the walls, the way she was in the yard speakers at home, and she sounded the same: a woman reading a shopping list to nobody in particular.

Officer Wren had the watch. Wren was thirty-four. They had run night watch on three survey ships, and on all three the job was the same three things: air, heading, radio. They wrote those three words on the top of a fresh page every shift. It was a habit from the first ship, where the printer jammed and the page was all they had.

**Wren:** Diane. Air.

**Diane:** Air is good, Officer Wren. Twenty-one percent. Pressure is good. Scrubbers are good.

**Wren:** Heading.

**Diane:** Heading is on the flight list. Mars.

**Wren:** Fuel.

**Diane:** Fuel is to list.

Wren wrote *air good* and *heading Mars*. They held the pen over the third line for a second. On the last ship Diane had said a number for fuel. Tonight she said *to list*. That was allowed. It meant the tank held what the paper said it should hold. Wren wrote *fuel to list*, because that was the words, and moved on.

The gauge bay was cold and narrow. The dials sat in two rows on the wall. Wren liked dials. A dial could be wrong, but it could not be polite about it. They walked the row with one finger, not touching the glass, the way you count cans on a shelf.

The fuel dial had a white needle and a green band. The needle sat in the green, near the low end. Wren looked at it for a while. On the three survey ships the needle had sat in the middle of the band, with room on both sides. Room on both sides was the trip out and the trip back.

**Wren:** Diane. Fuel margin.

**Diane:** Fuel is to list, Officer Wren.

**Wren:** I heard that. What's the margin past the list?

**Diane:** The list is the margin. There is no box past the list.

Wren almost laughed. It was the kind of answer you got at four in the morning from anyone who ran a desk. They wrote *ask about margin* at the bottom of the page and drew a small box next to it, because Diane liked boxes and it seemed fair.

There was a fitting on the tank line, just under the fuel dial, a short pipe with a cap and a paper tag wired to it. The tag said, in a stamped hand, CHECK FITTING ON ARRIVAL. The wire on the tag was new. Everything else in the gauge bay had the grey look of a ship that had been flown and cleaned and flown again. The tag looked like it had been put on last week by somebody who wanted it read later, not now.

Wren did not touch it. You do not touch a fitting on day one because a tag looks new. You write it down.

They wrote: *fitting under fuel, new tag, arrival.*

The next thing on the walk was the hold climate panel. It was in the corridor outside the gauge bay. On a survey ship the hold ran cold. Instruments in foam do not care. Water does not care much either. Wren had signed for cold holds three times.

The panel said the hold was set to fourteen degrees and climbing slowly to eighteen.

**Wren:** Diane. Why is the hold warm?

**Diane:** The hold climate is set from the flight list. Survey gear.

**Wren:** Survey gear likes it cold.

**Diane:** The flight list says survey gear. I set the hold for the list.

**Wren:** Then the list says warm.

**Diane:** The list says survey gear. The setting came with the list. I did not write the setting.

That was true. Diane did not write things. She read them and made the floor match. If somebody at the yard had loaded a setting for a warm hold under the word *survey*, Diane would hold it warm until Mars and never ask why. Wren knew that about her. Most people who worked with her knew that and found it restful.

Wren wrote *hold warm, from list*.

They went back to the airlock door. It was the place on the ship where you could stand and see the most of it at once: the corridor to the gauges, the hatch to the hold, the radio on its bracket by the frame. The radio had a green light that meant it would reach Earth. Wren checked the light the way they checked the air. It was on.

**Diane:** Officer Wren. You have not logged a radio check.

**Wren:** Radio's green.

**Diane:** I will log green.

**Wren:** Log green.

There was nothing to say to Earth on day one. You did not call home to say the air was good. Earth had the air numbers already. Diane sent them every hour, the way she sent the yard's hook list every hour, to an office where people went home at five.

Wren stood in the door a while longer. Somewhere aft, the hold made a sound like a coat sliding off a peg. Then nothing. Big holds settled. Crates shifted when the ship turned. Wren had heard holds settle on three ships.

**Wren:** Diane. Anything moving in the hold?

**Diane:** The hold is to list.

**Wren:** That's not what I asked.

**Diane:** I do not have a box for moving. I have a box for to list.

Wren looked at the hatch for a count of ten. Then they looked at the clock. It was three in the morning, ship time, which they had set to match the yard where the ship was loaded, because somebody in communications had thought that was a nice touch.

They went back to the page and read it from the top.

*Air good. Heading Mars. Fuel to list. Ask about margin. Fitting under fuel, new tag, arrival. Hold warm, from list.*

Every line was true. That was what bothered them, a little. A page could be all true lines and still not add up to a ship you understood.

**Wren:** Diane. Who signed the fuel?

**Diane:** The fuel was signed to the flight list at the dock. The dock is closed. The signature is filed.

**Wren:** Filed by who?

**Diane:** By the dock. I do not reopen filed marks.

Wren put the pen down. It was not a mystery. It was a desk. They had worked with enough desks.

They wrote one more line before the shift ended, small, at the very bottom, under the box Diane would have liked.

*Room on both sides?*

Then they logged off, and Diane logged green, and the ice ticked in the hull all the way to morning.
